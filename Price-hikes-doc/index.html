The Watt Cost

Electricity prices are rising nationwide as AI data centers compete for grid capacity. Low-income households, who already spend nearly three times more of their income on energy than everyone else, are taking a big hit.

The premise

A small number of households in Baltimore and DC neighborhoods carry this film. Families for whom a rising electric bill isn't an inconvenience but a genuine financial cliff. Their kitchens and their bills are where the story starts.

From there, it follows two threads outward, both converging on Washington: where the demand driving prices up is actually coming from, and why the safety net meant to catch these households is itself under strain. The region makes this possible and is home to some of the country's highest-burden low-income households, minutes from the data center corridor driving the price spike, and the seat of the federal decisions shaping the outcome.

Why now

28.8%

Rise in the average U.S. residential electricity bill from 2021 to 2025, with prices projected to keep climbing as data center demand strains the grid.[1]

7.2%

Share of income the average low-income household spends on energy which is roughly three times the share spent by the typical U.S. household.[2]

40%

Approximate share of a recent PJM capacity auction's total cost attributed to data center demand which is an auction that cleared at the regulatory price cap and still fell short of the grid's reliability target.[3]

$4.05B

LIHEAP funding preserved by Congress after a 2025 proposal to eliminate the program entirely. That funding is threatened again in the FY2027 budget.[4]
